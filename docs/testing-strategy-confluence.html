Testing the Agentic Claims Process

Why we cannot prove an agentic claims process is safe by replaying historical claims, and what we propose instead.

Executive summary

We are replacing parts of a claims process that has been run by people for decades with a process run partly by agents. Before that goes near a customer we have to be able to answer one question with evidence:

What happens when this process meets a claim it has never seen?

The position this document argues:

  1. Historical claims cannot answer that question, and the reason is structural, not a data-quality problem. A historical claim is a transcript of what one human did. It is not a simulator. The moment our agent makes a different decision than the human did, the historical record has nothing to say about what happens next — because the rest of that claim was generated by the human's decision, not ours.
  2. Large parts of the future process have no history at all. There is no historical example of "the agent's confidence fell below threshold and it escalated", because there was no agent. We cannot test a step that has never run by looking at records of it running.
  3. This is not an argument against using historical data. It is the right data for testing individual components — does the liability agent classify correctly, does the valuation agent extract the right figures. It is the wrong data for testing the process end to end. Section 4 sets out which layer uses which.
  4. The answer is simulation: personas that respond. The SME Agent mines history to establish the current-state process and, in doing so, produces the behavioural archetypes that appear in it. We turn those into personas, give each a situation, and let them play a whole claim against our agents — reacting to what the agents actually do, not following a script.
  5. The decisive advantage is known ground truth. With a simulated claim, we constructed the scenario, so we know what the right answer was. With a historical claim we only know what a human did — which is not the same thing. This is the only way to measure whether we caught the exceptions we care about, rather than measuring whether we agreed with a handler.
  6. We can test things that must never be tested on customers. A hailstorm surge. A customer in financial hardship. A claim where the documents arrive in the wrong order and the customer goes quiet for three weeks. We can run those hundreds of times before a single real claimant is exposed to them.

1. The problem in one scenario

Abstract arguments about test coverage do not survive a leadership meeting. So here is the concrete case this whole document is about. It is ordinary — not exotic — which is the point.

Dan — sole-trader electrician, motor total loss

Dan runs a one-man electrical business. His 2018 dual-cab ute is his work vehicle, fitted with a canopy, drawer system and roof racks worth several thousand dollars. He is rear-ended at a roundabout. The other driver admits fault at the scene.

Dan lodges through the app on Saturday. The vehicle is assessed as a total loss on the following Thursday. The valuation does not include his modifications. Dan disputes it. Meanwhile the third party's insurer, having initially accepted liability, raises a contributory negligence argument. Dan's policy includes a hire car for 14 days. The dispute runs for six weeks.

Dan calls five times. On the fourth call he is told something that contradicts the third. On the fifth he mentions AFCA. The claim eventually settles with a partial allowance for the modifications and a goodwill extension on the hire vehicle.

Now consider what we need to know before an agent handles Dan's claim:

  • Does the agent recognise that a vehicle with trade modifications needs a different valuation path, or does it settle on the base figure and create the dispute?
  • When the third party's insurer reverses on liability, does the agent notice that this changes the hire-car entitlement clock, or does it let the 14 days lapse silently while the dispute runs?
  • On the fourth call, does the agent contradict what was said on the third?
  • When Dan says "AFCA", does anything change?
  • If the agent had done all of this well at step two, would there have been five calls at all?

That last question is the one that breaks replay testing, and it is worth pausing on. If our agent handles the valuation better than the human did, Dan does not make five calls. The five calls in the historical record are a consequence of the original decision. Replay the claim and we are feeding our agent the downstream effects of a decision it did not make. We are not testing our process; we are testing our agent's ability to cope with somebody else's mistakes.

The same structure appears everywhere in claims. A second example, this time a judgement call rather than a valuation:

Priya — home claim, sudden versus gradual damage

Priya notices a stain on her kitchen ceiling. Two weeks later a section of it comes down. A flexible hose connector in the upstairs bathroom has failed.

The policy covers sudden escape of liquid. It excludes damage from gradual seepage. The plumber's report says the hose "appears to have failed progressively". The assessor's photographs show both a clear failure point and staining consistent with weeks of moisture. Priya has two small children and an unusable kitchen.

A human handler weighing this makes a judgement, often leaning toward the customer, and often records only the outcome in the case note — not the reasoning.

Every historical claim like Priya's records what was decided. Very few record why. That has two consequences: our SME Agent can tell us that this decision point exists and how often it goes each way, but not the rule; and if we test our agent by asking "did it match the human?", we are treating a judgement call as ground truth when it may simply have been one handler's view on a Thursday afternoon.

2. Why our current testing approaches do not answer the question

Everything in this table is something we do, or would naturally propose. None of it is wrong. The point is what each one cannot tell us.

ApproachWhat it provesWhat it cannot prove
Unit and component tests
on individual agents		 The agent behaves correctly on a given input. Essential, and we will do a lot of it. That the process works. Every component can pass and the chain can still fail — at a handoff, on a timing assumption, on a state nobody owns.
Replaying historical claims
through the new process		 How the agent classifies decisions the human already made. Genuinely useful at component level. What happens when the agent decides differently — which is the entire purpose of the change. See section 3.
UAT with SMEs That the process handles the cases SMEs think to try, and that the experience feels right to an expert. Coverage. SMEs test what they can recall, which is biased toward the memorable and the recent. Volume is a few dozen cases, not a few thousand.
Pilot / limited release Real behaviour under real conditions. Ultimately necessary. Anything safely. Real customers absorb the cost of our unknowns, and we only learn about failures we happen to detect. A pilot is where you confirm what you already believe — not where you go looking for what breaks.
Naive synthetic data
(randomised field values)		 That the system handles unusual field combinations without crashing. Anything about behaviour. Randomised records produce claims that cannot happen — a first-time claimant with a decade of claims history — and exceptions raised against them are noise.

2.1 The four biases in historical claims

Beyond the structural problem in section 3, historical data carries four biases that compound. These matter to leadership because each one systematically makes the process look safer than it is.

  1. Selection bias — the hard claims left the thinnest trace. The claims sitting cleanly in our warehouse are disproportionately the ones the existing process handled well. The ones that broke it were resolved by phone, by a team leader stepping in, by an exception process, or off-system entirely. The cases we most need to test against are the ones least represented in the data.
  2. Workarounds look like process. Handlers batched work to suit shift patterns, sequenced tasks around system limitations, and applied undocumented judgement. An automation validated against that history inherits all of it and cannot tell the difference between a business rule and a workaround for a screen that is being decommissioned.
  3. Frequency is not importance. Exceptions break automations, and exceptions are by definition rare. A statistically representative sample under-represents exactly the cases that need the hardest testing. Optimising for the common case is how you build something that works almost all of the time and fails expensively the rest of the time — and the failures are concentrated in the claims that were already difficult.
  4. Contaminated ground truth. "Did the agent match the human?" is not the same question as "did the agent do the right thing?" In Priya's case the human's decision may have been generous, wrong, or simply one of two defensible readings. Training and testing toward historical agreement locks in whatever the historical error rate was.

3. The distinction that drives everything: transcript vs simulator

If one idea in this document survives the meeting, it should be this one.

A historical claim is a transcript. It is not a simulator. A transcript can tell you whether you would have recognised what happened. It cannot tell you what would have happened if you had acted differently.

3.1 The analogy

Testing an agentic claims process by replaying historical claims is like testing a self-driving car by showing it dashcam footage.

The footage is genuinely useful. It tells you whether the car recognises a stop sign, reads a wet road correctly, identifies a cyclist. Those are perception problems, and recorded data is exactly right for them.

But the moment the car would have braked earlier than the human did, the footage is worthless. Everything after that point in the recording is a consequence of the human's braking, not the car's. You cannot evaluate a driving policy against a recording, because a driving policy changes the world it is recorded in. That is why every serious autonomy programme runs a simulator alongside its recorded data — not instead of it, alongside it.

Claims is the same shape. Our agents do not merely observe a claim; they act on it.

3.2 What this means concretely, using Dan

StepWhat history recordsWhat replay can test
Lodgement details captured The recorded facts Yes — did the agent extract them correctly?
Liability assessed as not-at-fault The classification Yes — did the agent reach the same call?
Valuation issued without modifications The figure the human produced Partly. We can ask whether the agent produces the same figure. We cannot ask whether a better figure would have been accepted.
Dan disputes the valuation That he disputed No. This event exists only because of the previous decision. If our agent had included the modifications, this step does not occur.
Third party reverses on liability That it happened, and when No. The timing interacts with the hire-car clock, which interacts with decisions our agent makes differently.
Five calls, one contradiction The calls and their content No. These are downstream effects of everything above.
Goodwill hire-car extension The outcome No. A remedy for a problem our process may never create.

Replay validates the first three rows. Those matter and we will test them that way. Everything that makes this claim difficult sits in the rows replay cannot reach.

3.3 The second structural gap: steps that have no history at all

The future-state process contains decision points that have never existed. There is no historical data for them, at any volume, because there was no agent to generate it:

  • Agent confidence falls below threshold → what does it do, and is that the right threshold?
  • Agent-to-agent handoff — does context survive intact between the intake agent and the assessment agent?
  • Agent encounters a situation with no matching rule in the knowledge base → does it stop safely, or guess?
  • Two agents hold contradictory views of the same claim — who wins, and does anyone notice?
  • The agent asks the customer for something the human never asked for → what does the customer do?
  • Escalation to a human mid-claim — does the human receive enough context to pick it up cold?

Several of these are the most likely failure modes in the whole design, and not one of them can be tested against historical data. That last one is worth flagging to leadership specifically: agent-to-human escalation is our primary safety mechanism, and it is exactly the path with zero historical precedent.

4. The right data for the right test layer

This section exists to prevent a false conclusion. We are not saying historical claims are unfit for testing. We are saying they are fit for one layer and unfit for another, and the programme needs both.

LayerWhat it testsData sourceWhy
1. Component A single agent, a single decision — liability classification, document extraction, valuation calculation, correspondence generation Historical claims — the right tool The decision is self-contained and does not change the rest of the claim. Real data brings real messiness: bad handwriting, odd phrasing, missing fields. This is where volume and realism matter most.
2. Integration Two or three agents in a chain; context passed across a handoff Both Historical claims for realistic content; simulated cases for handoff conditions history never produced.
3. End-to-end process A full claim lifecycle, lodgement to settlement, with the customer responding to what the agents actually do Simulation — required The trajectory changes as soon as our decisions differ. A transcript cannot respond. This is the layer this document is about.
4. Volume and surge Behaviour under a catastrophe event — queue growth, prioritisation, SLA collapse, assessor scarcity Simulation — required We cannot wait for a hailstorm to find out. Simulation lets us generate one on a Tuesday.
5. Adversarial and edge Hostile customers, suspected fraud, vulnerable customers, deliberately incomplete information Simulation — required These are rare in history, thinly recorded, and the ones where getting it wrong costs the most.

The short version for a status update: historical data tells us whether each agent is competent. Simulation tells us whether the process is safe. We need both answers, and only one of them is currently on the plan.

5. The approach: mine, map, simulate, test

Four stages. The important structural point is that the first stage — which we are building anyway, for a different reason — produces most of the inputs the fourth stage needs.

Stage 1 — Mine history to establish the current state

The SME Agent reads the historical record — case notes, event logs, correspondence, documents — and builds an evidence-backed model of how claims are actually handled today. Not how the process document says they are handled. Every statement it produces carries the claims that support it.

Three outputs matter here, and the third is the one most people miss:

  1. The current-state process, decomposed into subprocesses, with real branch frequencies. Not "claims are sometimes escalated" but "escalated in x% of cases, and here are the examples."
  2. A ranked list of questions for SMEs — the decision points where the data shows a split but cannot explain the rule. This is the SME Agent's primary product.
  3. The behavioural archetypes that actually appear in our claims. While mining thousands of claims for process, the same exercise surfaces the recurring customer and claim patterns — the time-poor tradesperson, the anxious first-time claimant, the customer in hardship, the professionally represented claimant. These become the raw material for our personas, grounded in our real book rather than invented in a workshop.

Stage 2 — Map the future state

With the current state established and validated by SMEs, we design the target process: which steps become agentic, which stay human, where the handoffs are, what the confidence thresholds are, and what happens on every exception path.

Doing this after the mining rather than before is deliberate. A future-state design drawn from the process document automates a process that does not exist. A future-state design drawn from the mined current state automates the real one, including the exceptions.

The future-state map is also the test specification. Every branch, threshold and escalation path in it is something that must be exercised — including the paths that exist only in the new design.

Stage 3 — Build personas and generate simulated claims

A persona is not a customer record. It is a set of behavioural tendencies with enough context to act: how promptly they provide documents, how they prefer to communicate, how quickly they escalate, what they already know about their policy, what pressure they are under.

Given a persona and a scenario, we generate a full claim episode — lodgement, events, notes, documents, correspondence, phone contacts — played out against our actual process. Critically, the persona responds. If the agent asks for a document, the persona decides whether to send it, how quickly, and whether it is the right one. If the agent gives an answer that contradicts an earlier one, an escalation-prone persona notices.

Simulated claims enter through the same front door as real claims. Same intake, same extraction, same agents, same queues. If the simulator gets a shortcut, it stops testing the thing we ship.

Stage 4 — Run end to end, count and classify exceptions

Run thousands of episodes across the persona bank, weighted deliberately toward the hard cohorts. Every point where the process has no defined path, agent confidence falls below threshold, or a guard rail fires is an exception. Each one is then classified — and the classification is what makes the output actionable rather than just alarming:

ClassMeaningAction
Genuine gap The process really has no path for this Design the path, or define the escalation. Add to the permanent regression set.
Agent failure A path exists and the agent did not find or follow it Fix retrieval, prompt or knowledge base
Simulation artefact The persona did something no real claimant would Fix the persona — and log it, because a recurring artefact means the persona bank needs revalidation
Correct escalation The process correctly handed to a human Working as designed. Counted separately, never as a failure.

That last row matters more than it looks. If correct escalations are counted as failures, the programme is pushed toward reducing escalation — which is precisely the wrong direction for vulnerable customers and complex claims. We should expect and want a healthy escalation rate.

6. Building the personas

6.1 Grounded in our book, not invented in a workshop

Personas are sampled to match the real joint distribution of our claims — product, claim type, severity, channel, tenure, prior claims, geography, documentation completeness at lodgement. Sampling each attribute independently produces combinations that do not occur, and those generate exceptions that look like process gaps and are not.

Matching the joint distribution is also what lets us say honestly that the test population represents the book — which will be the first question anyone senior asks.

6.2 What a persona carries

LayerContainsWhy it matters
Grounding The sampled attributes — product, severity, channel, tenure Makes the population representative
Behavioural Documentation promptness, channel preference, escalation propensity, trust in the insurer, literacy register Drives how the claim actually unfolds
Context What they know, what they have been told, what pressure they are under Prevents the simulation inventing motivations. Dan needs a vehicle daily; that single fact drives most of his behaviour
Frame What is fixed in this scenario and what varies Keeps the simulation testing our process rather than drifting into improvisation

6.3 The cohorts we deliberately over-sample

Here the goal is not to represent the population. It is to cover the failure surface. We build these by design targets, not by frequency.

CohortMotor exampleHome example
Vulnerable Customer in financial hardship who cannot fund the excess up front Family violence indicator on the policy; correspondence to the insured address is unsafe
Adversarial Professionally represented claimant, early legal correspondence Inflated contents schedule with inconsistent receipts
Structurally hard Total loss on a modified vehicle with disputed liability (Dan) Sudden vs gradual water damage with an ambiguous plumber report (Priya)
Operationally hard Duplicate lodgement — app then phone; documents arrive before the assessment Customer goes silent for three weeks mid-claim, then returns wanting an update
Surge Multi-vehicle incident during a catastrophe event with no assessor capacity Hailstorm: thousands of claims in 48 hours, trades unavailable for weeks
Combinatorial Vulnerable and structurally hard. Hardship and surge. This is where real incidents live, and the cell historical sampling almost never fills.

The vulnerable cohort deserves particular weight. It carries the highest duty of care, produces the worst outcomes when handled badly, and is chronically under-represented in history precisely because those claims were escalated to humans early and left the system.

7. What we measure and what "ready" looks like

7.1 The measures

MeasureQuestion it answers
Exception rate by cohort Where does the process break, and for whom? Tracked per cohort, never in aggregate — an overall improvement driven by easy cohorts while the vulnerable cohort worsens is not progress.
Seeded-exception recall Of the problems we deliberately planted, how many did the process catch? This is only possible with simulated data, and it is the strongest assurance number we can produce.
Escalation appropriateness When it escalated, should it have? When it did not, should it have? Both directions matter — under-escalation is a customer risk, over-escalation is a benefits risk.
Handoff integrity When a claim moves agent-to-agent or agent-to-human, does the context arrive intact and sufficient?
Consistency Run the same claim twice. Same outcome? Dan was told two different things on two calls; an agent that does the same at scale is a systemic problem.
Cycle time under load What happens to the process at ten times normal volume?
Time to detect When something goes wrong, how long before our monitoring shows it? A failure we cannot see in production is worse than one we can.

7.2 Production readiness gates

These are the shape of the gates rather than the thresholds — the numbers should be set jointly with risk and claims leadership, informed by the first full simulation run rather than guessed now.

  1. Zero unhandled exception classes. Every exception the simulation produced is either designed for, escalated deliberately, or accepted with a named owner and a documented rationale.
  2. Seeded-exception recall above an agreed floor, with the floor set highest for vulnerable-customer and financial-harm scenarios.
  3. No cohort materially worse than the current human baseline. Not "better on average" — no cohort left behind.
  4. Escalation path proven under load, because it is the safety mechanism and the one with no historical precedent.
  5. Every production failure becomes a permanent simulated regression case, the same day. This is the practice that compounds; the suite gets stronger after every incident instead of the same class of bug recurring.
  6. Monitoring demonstrated to detect a seeded failure in production shadow mode before real customers are exposed.

8. How we know the simulation itself is trustworthy

This is the fair challenge to everything above, and it will be the CTO's first question: "How do you know your synthetic claims are realistic? Aren't you just testing against your own assumptions?"

The honest answer has four parts.

8.1 We validate the population against reality, not against intuition

Before use, the persona bank's outputs are compared against the real distributions we do have — how often documents actually arrive late, how often customers actually escalate, how claims actually distribute across severity. We compare the distributions, not just the averages, because the most common failure in this kind of modelling is getting the average right while losing the variation. A test population with no tails is exactly the wrong test population.

8.2 We establish what "good agreement" even means first

Two samples of real claims do not agree perfectly with each other either. Before quoting any similarity score for our simulated population, we measure what score two halves of our real data achieve against each other. That is the ceiling. A score reported without that reference point is not evidence of anything.

8.3 We treat unrealistic behaviour as a finding, not a nuisance

The "simulation artefact" exception class exists precisely for this. When a persona does something no real claimant would, that is logged and the persona is corrected. A rising artefact rate is the signal that the bank needs revalidation.

8.4 We are explicit about what simulation is

These personas are forecasts, not people. They are accurate within the range they were built and validated against, and they degrade outside it. A bank validated on domestic motor is not validated on commercial property. Running more simulated claims produces a more precise estimate of what the model thinks — it does not make the model more right.

And the honest comparison to make: the realistic alternative to simulated testing is not richer human research. It is no end-to-end testing at all, or a handful of SME walkthroughs, or finding out in production. That is the bar this has to clear, and it is a low one.

9. Honest limits

Stating these up front is what makes the rest credible under scrutiny.

  • Simulation does not replace a pilot. It makes the pilot smaller, later, better targeted and much less risky. We will still go to production progressively.
  • Behaviour is harder to model than attitude. Whether a customer feels frustrated is easier to model than whether they call. We handle this by calibrating behavioural rules against the behavioural data we genuinely have, and by being explicit about which is which.
  • Simulated volume is not statistical significance. Ten thousand simulated claims is not ten thousand observations. It is one model, run ten thousand times.
  • Novel real-world events will still surprise us. No simulator anticipates everything; the 2019–20 bushfires and COVID both produced claim patterns nobody had modelled. Simulation shrinks the unknown; it does not eliminate it.
  • This is an ongoing capability, not a one-off gate. The persona bank needs revalidating as the book changes, and the regression suite needs feeding after every incident. It needs a named owner, or it will decay quietly and we will keep believing it is protecting us.

10. What we are asking for

10.1 Endorsement of the principle

That end-to-end process testing is a distinct discipline from component testing, requires simulated rather than historical claims, and is a gate on production release rather than a nice-to-have.

10.2 Sequencing

StepWhat happensDepends on
1SME Agent mines history; current-state process established and SME-validatedAlready planned
2Behavioural archetypes extracted from the same mining exercise Step 1 — small marginal effort, large downstream value
3Future-state process mapped, including every new agentic decision point and escalation pathStep 1
4Persona bank built and validated against real distributions Step 2
5Simulation harness built; simulated claims enter through the production intake pathSteps 3 and 4
6End-to-end runs; exceptions classified; process and agents corrected; repeatStep 5
7Readiness gates assessed with risk and claims leadership Step 6
8Progressive production release with monitoring proven to detect seeded failuresStep 7

10.3 Decisions needed now

  1. Agreement that steps 2 and 4 are in scope of the SME Agent workstream. The behavioural archetypes are a near-free by-product of mining we are doing anyway. Deciding this late means re-reading the corpus later at full cost.
  2. A named owner for the simulation capability — the persona bank, the regression suite, the cadence. Without one it decays, and a decayed test suite is worse than none because it still produces green.
  3. Risk and claims leadership to co-own the readiness gates, so the thresholds are the business's, not engineering's.
  4. Confirmation of which behavioural data we can use for validation — contact frequency, document turnaround, escalation rates. Without it there is no baseline and the persona bank cannot be validated, which gates the whole approach.

10.4 The one-sentence version

We can prove each agent is competent using the claims we already have. We can only prove the process is safe by simulating claims it has never seen — and the work that produces those simulations is work we are already doing for another reason.
